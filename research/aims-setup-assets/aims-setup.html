Building an Acoustic Intensity Measurement System for Ultrasound Pressure-Field Characterization

Designing and building a hydrophone-based measurement rig into a fully verified acquisition chain: integrating a precision digitizer, a 3-axis motorized stage, and a calibrated hydrophone, then diagnosing and resolving the hardware and software failures standing between an assembled system and one ready to run a measurement.

Faria Rahman · Hydrophone-based pressure-field measurement setup · 2026

Overview

Why measure the pressure field at all

Before an ultrasound transducer can be trusted for imaging or therapy research, its actual acoustic output has to be measured, not assumed. An Acoustic Intensity Measurement System (AIMS) does this by scanning a calibrated hydrophone through the beam in a water tank and recording pressure as a function of position, which in turn yields standard safety and performance metrics — spatial-peak pulse-average intensity (ISPPA), spatial-peak temporal-average intensity (ISPTA), mechanical index (MI), and peak rarefactional pressure.

I designed and built this measurement setup: developing the system schematic, integrating the hardware — transducer, hydrophone, motorized stage, and digitizer — into a single acquisition chain, and verifying every subsystem along the way, replacing the parts that didn't work until the full chain was ready for an actual measurement campaign.

This page documents that process honestly: what was designed, what was built, what was verified, and what broke and how it got fixed. The acquisition chain itself is fully built, integrated, and verified end-to-end; running the hydrophone pressure-field scan itself is future work.

System

Hardware stack and signal chain

The measurement chain: a Verasonics Vantage system drives an L11-5v linear array transducer submerged in a water tank; a calibrated hydrophone, mounted on a 3-axis motorized stage on the opposite side of the tank, picks up the resulting pressure wave; the signal is amplified and digitized, and the same Verasonics system provides the trigger so acquisition is synchronized to each transmitted pulse.

  • Ultrasound source: Verasonics Vantage system, L11-5v linear array transducer
  • Hydrophone: Onda HGL-0085 needle hydrophone with AG-2010 preamplifier
  • Positioning: Sigma Koki SHOT-304GS 3-axis motorized stage (RS-232C serial control)
  • Digitizer: GaGe CompuScope CSE123G2-2GS, up to 3 GS/s per channel
  • Control software: a legacy LabVIEW 2016 (32-bit) VI, plus a MATLAB pipeline built as a more maintainable alternative

Assembly

Mechanical integration

Getting accurate pressure-field data starts with precise, repeatable alignment. The hydrophone and transducer mounts use ThorLabs micrometer translation stages for fine manual positioning, on top of the SigmaKoki motorized stage that handles the programmed raster scan. Assembling and aligning this hardware — and understanding exactly how each translation and rotation stage interacts with the others — was a prerequisite for everything downstream.

The stage geometry turned out to be non-uniform across axes — different DIVIDE and base-rate settings on the SigmaKoki controller give different physical distances per pulse on each axis — which had to be worked out from the controller manual before trusting any programmed move.

Verification

Confirming the digitizer independently, before trusting the full chain

Rather than debugging the whole system at once, the GaGe digitizer was verified in isolation first: a bench function generator (Tektronix AFG31000) drove a known 1 MHz, 50 mVpp sine wave into a 50 Ω load, cross-checked live on a Teledyne LeCroy WaveRunner 640Zi oscilloscope, and the same signal was fed into the GaGe card and inspected in GageScope — GaGe's own acquisition-and-display software, which serves the same role here as an oscilloscope on the PC. A clean, matching waveform in GageScope confirmed the digitizer hardware and driver chain were sound before wiring it into the full measurement setup.

Build Log

Diagnosing and fixing four independent failures

The system was inherited in a non-working state, and each subsystem had its own, unrelated failure. Isolating them — rather than assuming one root cause — was most of the work.

01

LabVIEW VI wouldn't compile — 34 broken sub-VIs. The error list traced every failure to ROOT CAUSE: Dependency is broken on the CsLv_* digitizer calls, meaning the GaGe CompuScope driver simply wasn't installed on that machine. A fresh driver install resolved all 34 errors at once.

02

The 3-axis stage wouldn't move. Windows had no working driver for the USB-to-serial adapter linking the SigmaKoki motion controller to the PC, so LabVIEW's motion commands went nowhere. Installing the correct driver and confirming a clean COM port in Device Manager fixed it — then confirmed independently with a MATLAB serialport() connection at 9600 baud.

03

"Invalid segment size" aborted every acquisition. At first this looked like a LabVIEW bug. It's actually a hardware/firmware memory-granularity constraint on the CompuScope card itself — reproducible outside LabVIEW too. Sweeping candidate values empirically showed 8,320 samples succeeds where 8,192 fails at a 250 MS/s sample rate.

04

Stage motion units didn't mean what they looked like. LabVIEW issues moves in micrometers, not motor pulses, and each axis converts those differently depending on its DIVIDE and base-rate settings — decoded from the SHOT-304GS manual rather than assumed, after finding the stage's resolution isn't uniform across all three axes.

Status

What's built, and what's next

Verified & working
  • GaGe digitizer confirmed acquiring a known signal end-to-end (function generator → digitizer → GageScope)
  • 3-axis SigmaKoki stage confirmed responsive to programmed moves, both via LabVIEW and independently via MATLAB serial commands
  • LabVIEW control VI runs cleanly with zero dependency errors
  • Transducer and hydrophone mechanically mounted, aligned, and facing each other across the tank
  • A parallel MATLAB pipeline replicating the full LabVIEW pipeline (acquisition, stage control, intensity calculations) built and analytically self-tested
Future Work
